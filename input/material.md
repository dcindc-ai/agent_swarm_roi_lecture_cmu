# Material for the council

## The lecture
Guest lecture on AI ROI for a public-policy graduate class (Heinz 94704, Building the AI Organization), 24 Nov 2026, 35 minutes, 12 to 20 students. Spine: AI is infrastructure that is grown, not built. The science is outrunning our ability to measure it, and our ability to use it.

## Slide titles (accessible deck, 14 slides)
1. A crop with a bridge contract
2. The seed changes every few weeks. One agency already grades it every day.
3. The meter is not the harvest
4. The water bill is not the bill
5. Price per token falls. Tokens per task rise. Carry both.
6. Better than what? The yield is measured against last season
7. Who grades the crop? Never the seed company.
8. When do you stop watering? Manage the slope and set the decision date.
9. True in the catalog, different in the field
10. What is good enough? One row per dimension, signed before the money moves
11. Outrunning our ability to use it
12. What you are taking home: the calculator, pre-loaded

## The question each slide asks the room
- Which of our procurement steps assume the thing stays as we bought it?
- When the model changes, who re-scores it, and is that in the contract?
- What is the unit of work we are paying for, and who checks whether it was any good?
- What share of our cost per accepted output is the token bill, and per seat or per token, which is cheaper for the way we will actually use it?
- At half the token price and twice the tokens per task, does this case still hold, and can the contracting officer get us a short term or a price-adjustment clause?
- What is our baseline, measured before the buy, by whoever runs the current process, and is the vendor’s number reported against it?
- Who measures capability, do they sell it, and would this vendor be scored on data it has never seen?
- What is the decision date, and what slope triggers a pivot?
- Where was this measured, on whose data, and what was the mission number?
- For each row, who signs, and what did they sign before the test?
- Where did the freed hours go, by name, and who redesigned the job?
- Which assumption made the answer look that good?

## Syllabus excerpt
# Building the AI Organization (Heinz 94704), syllabus overview (sample, revised 01/06/26)
Program: Managing AI Systems. Faculty: Karl J Maschino, Distinguished Service Professor, Heinz College. 7 units. Zoom class, weekday evening ET.

Course description: AI transforms agility, decision-making and competitiveness, but building an AI-enabled organization requires coordinated transformation across people, processes and strategy. Frameworks for transforming an organization into an AI-enabled enterprise across strategy, structure, governance, risk, financial management, change management and workforce development. Actionable tools to evaluate current state, identify AI adoption opportunities, and build a roadmap toward sustainable value creation with AI.

Learning objectives: evaluate organizational structure and apply systems thinking to AI implementation; core principles of business strategy applied to AI transformation; integrate risk management fundamentals (bias, explainability, regulatory exposure) with enterprise risk frameworks; workforce planning (upskilling, reskilling, talent sourcing); change management models; governance models for AI systems; evaluate financial management and budgeting challenges specific to AI (cost of experimentation, vendor evaluation, long-term scaling); create a comprehensive roadmap for an AI-ready organization.

Instructor: CFO, CAO and Co-Chief Risk Officer of GAO 2014 to 2024 (agency of about 3,500 staff); earlier DOJ budget analyst (PMF), Criminal Division executive officer (SES), appropriations liaison, OMB program examiner; Heinz MPM; Certified Government Financial Manager; NAPA Fellow.

Course topics in order:
1. Introduction to Building an AI Organization: scope; pitfalls and promise of the AI revolution; evaluate current structure; systems thinking for AI implementation.
2. Understanding your Organization: evaluate structure; challenges of implementing an AI initiative.
3. Building AI Strategy: business strategy principles; describe your AI initiative and its alignment with competitive strategy; strategic implications.
4. Risk Management: integrate risk management fundamentals into AI strategic planning.
5. Organizations and Talent Management, Building the AI Team: workforce planning, upskilling, reskilling, talent sourcing.
6. Change Management: why a change strategy matters for any AI initiative; compare models; leadership.
7. Implementing AI Governance and Policy: governance strategy tailored to the organization; theory and practice.
8. Data Governance and Strategy: governance models for AI systems and organizational structures.
9. Financial Management and AI Systems (the week before the guest session): articulate the financial complexities of an AI initiative; key factors from a financial perspective.
10. Building Roadmaps: review of previous module presentations; roadmap presentation; open Q&A; work on final paper.
11. Project Presentations: idea sharing; Q&A; submit final projects; wrap-up.

Students: 12 to 20 graduate students, future public-sector managers (not engineers); final deliverable is a roadmap for an AI-ready organization. The guest session (Dave Cook, 24 Nov 2026, 30 to 45 minutes) sits after Financial Management and before Building Roadmaps.
